How It Works

Four steps, fully auditable

  1. Start with last year. Visa demand is strongly seasonal — academic calendars, application cycles, and travel patterns repeat year to year — so last year's same month is a solid starting guess before any adjustment.
  2. Add real signals. GDP growth, job openings, and inflation shift that baseline up or down.
  3. Retrain every month. New actuals get added and the model refits automatically.
  4. Forecast and test scenarios. Get 3–12 month projections, or run what-ifs before deciding.
Why Macroeconomics

What moves visa demand besides the calendar

  • GDP growth — a stronger U.S. economy raises employer demand for foreign talent and pulls in more business and visitor travel; a slowdown does the reverse.
  • Job vacancy levels — more unfilled positions push employers to sponsor foreign workers, directly raising employment-based NIV demand.
  • Inflation — shapes discretionary travel spending and signals broader economic conditions that affect both U.S. and origin-country decisions to travel or relocate.

Bottom Line

Why the hybrid model wins

  • Normal months: modest edge — 9.6% error vs. 10.1% for naive. Most months are already close to last year, so there's only so much room to improve.
  • During a shock: the gap widens sharply — 76% error vs. 426% for naive across the COVID collapse and recovery.
  • Why: the naive baseline is structurally blind to anything new — it just repeats last year, so it stays wrong for a full 12 months no matter what happens. The hybrid model absorbs real signals (GDP, jobs, inflation) and corrects itself every month as actuals come in.

Data Sources

  • State Dept. NIV issuance statistics (monthly)
  • BLS job openings (JOLTS-style)
  • CPI inflation, monthly
  • GDP level, quarterly (expanded to monthly)

Model

  • Seasonal baseline + ridge regression residual
  • Runs entirely client-side — no server required
  • Retrains live as data is added
